I recently finished [*The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*](https://www.amazon.com/Five-Dysfunctions-Team-Leadership-Fable/dp/0787960756) by Patrick Lencioni while was also trying to digest the dense first chapters of [*Systemantics*](https://www.amazon.com/Systemantics-Systems-Work-Especially-They/dp/0812906748) by John Gall. To my restless mind, it was impossible not to see the connections between them. Ideas and scenarios seemed to jump off the page, forming another set of self-diagnoses about *why, so often, things just don’t work as we would like to*.

I’m far from holding any grand truth or carrying the staff of enlightenment. I’m just walking in the shallow shores of these vast topics. And while this isn’t a universal truth for every team or system, I believe some of it may resonate with your own reality.

So join me in this humble reflection, where I share my own perspective on how teams invest their energy — and why it doesn’t always flow into the outcomes we hope for.

## The treadmill

Have you ever sat through a week packed with meetings, sprint reviews, and endless updates — only to realize nothing actually changed? It feels like inefficiency, but it is actually something deeper: **anergy**.

Yes, ***anergy***.

Picture a treadmill running at full speed in an empty gym. The motor hums, the belt spins, electricity flows — but nobody is getting fitter. Teams can fall into the same trap: ***outputs*** are celebrated, ***activity is visible***, but the actual outcomes — the ***results that matter — never arrive***. Anergy here is meant to describe this exact dysfunction: energy is consumed, but nothing useful emerges.

**And here’s the dangerous part**: Teams look busy, dashboards glow with activity, calendars overflow with meetings. Yet, beneath all that motion, the ***system is stuck***.

## The word System

A “***system***” is not just software or tools. It’s the **routines**, actions, and **behaviors** that come together to ***form living entities*** we call **systems**.

Democracy, ***agile frameworks*** and even the balance between ***order*** and ***chaos*** — all are systems. They ***exists to*** make life easier. At least, that’s the idea.

**Until** they don’t.

Systems aim to ***efficiently solve problems*** by using energy toward a common goal. A good system produces valuable **outputs** (reports, features, decisions, better systems) that lead to meaningful **outcomes** (reliable operations, happier customers, stronger trust).

**The catch**? When using systems, many teams never bridge the gap. They generate ***outputs***, but the ***outcomes*** never materialize.

*Not in time*.

## The phenomenon of Anergy

This treadmill effect has a name. In ***Systemantics***, John Gall used ***anergy*** to describe how systems drift into a ***quiet paralysis***: activity continues, energy is spent, but nothing of **value emerges**.

The word itself comes also from physics, where ***anergy*** refers to the portion of energy in a system that cannot be converted into useful work. Energy is present, but it’s locked away, unavailable for transformation.

In software, for example, anergy shows up when CPU cycles are burned in loops that return nothing or when teams are sprinting but outcomes never arrive. ***A system that looks alive but delivers no value — that’s anergy in action***.

> A coiled spring is full of energy. When fully uncoiled, it is full of anergy.

## Symptoms of anergy in teams

From my perspective, anergy doesn’t come from laziness or inefficiency. More often, it arises from misalignment.

In his book, Patrick Lencioni described *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*. Seen through the lens of **anergy**, each dysfunction is not only a cultural or structural weakness, but also a point where team energy is **dissipated instead of converted into outcomes**.

I have adapted **_Lencioni’s_ model** to show how these **dysfunctions** map into forms of **anergy** within teams:

> **Absence of trust**. *When team members don’t trust one another, energy that could be spent solving problems is instead spent on self-protection by using guarding words and hiding vulnerabilities. There is motionbut no progress*.

> **Fear of conflict**. *Healthy debate generates momentum. But when conflict is avoided, teams burn cycles in polite but empty discussions. The energy is there, but it’s trapped, never channeled into real decisions*.

> **Lack of commitment**. *When the team isn’t really bought in, the plan changes with whoever shouts the loudest that day. People jump from one idea to the next, always busy but never really moving*.

> **Avoidance of accountability**. _If no one owns **outcomes**, individuals optimise for themselves. Each molecule moves in its own direction; the system loses coherence. The collective goal vanishes_.

> **Inattention to results**. _When the main goal is ignored, **outputs** replace **outcomes**. Dragging tickets, tweaking presentations, having “status” conversations. It feels like progress, but it doesn’t change anything that matters_.

It **doesn’t mean** that every team will carry all five dysfunctions at the same time. In my experience, these ***dysfunctions*** ***tend to shift and fluctuate*** with the team’s overall mood and context. Some weeks **trust** is high but **commitment** is low, other weeks **alignment** is strong but **accountability** slips.

## Shifting from anergy to energy

***Anergy isn’t forever***. It fades when teams *stop* **chasing** **outputs** and *start* **aligning on outcomes**.

What **makes** the **difference** isn’t radical change or big restructures. It’s the **small habits** — checking in, ***clarifying outcomes***, ***holding each other accountable***. These are the **practices** that keep energy from leaking away. Without them, even the most talented **team** **drifts** into busyness **without** **progress**.

It’s not about adding more effort. It’s about pointing the energy you already have in the right direction — together. **That’s when anergy becomes energy again**.
